DESCRIPTION and MANUAL

CoNDiP is a combined analytical dictionary and concordance of Nahuatl texts. It can exist only on computers; and it will be forever incomplete: more roots, words, comments, and texts are continually added. Entirely new elements may also be introduced.

The Description & Manual is written by an ignoramus within the realm of electronics; in spite of my enthusiasm for the use of computers, I have retained a naive attitude towards the machines and their entrails.

However, thanks to Michael Thomsen's useful and elucidating comments and corrections, the Description presents - I believe - a faithful picture of how both the system and its useful features are organized.

The system is offered in subscription and distributed on disks. The Description & Manual and CoNDiP Primer are sent out for free to all subscribers with version 2.1, November 1992. From then on new versions will be distributed every six to twelve months.

The project has been funded by the Carlsberg Foundation.

1. Introductory



Aim and basic organization

CoNDiP is a tool for cracking the use and meaning of words in Nahuatl texts. It provides immediate connections between texts and words, and between words and roots.

It organizes the material, and facilitates studies of how words are used in texts, what words are formed with what roots, etc.

It does not necessarily provide elegant text translations nor even adequate selections of glosses for roots and words.

CoNDiP organizes and suggests, but interpretation is restricted to an absolute minimum.

It is made up of three interconnected components: Root Dictionary, Word Dictionary, Text Component.

Within the two Dictionary components the entities are word and root, respectively. In the text component the entity is text, divided into sentences.

You may look up a word or a root, or you may ask to have a text presented on the screen.

The interactiveness consists in the links between the components and in the effortless movement between them:

ROOTS user

WORDS user

feeding

TEXTS

feeding



From a root entry in the Root Dictionary you may select a word containing the root and shift to its entry in the Word Dictionary.

From a word entry in the Word Dictionary you may either select one of the roots contained in the word and go to its entry in the Root Dictionary, or you may select a sentence containing the word and go to the Text Component to see the text containing that sentence.

In the Text Component you may select any word in any text and shift to the Word Dictionary to see that word's entry.

The three components are all continually and concurrently active, and it is possible at any time to shift between the Components in order to see the screen picture you have last selected in one of the other two Components.

CoNDiP can exist only on computers: the entries in the two dictionary components and the texts are important, and they could be printed in three or more books, but it is the connections, the linking, between roots and words and between words in the Word Dictionary and word forms in the texts that make CoNDiP the tool it is; and those connections, that interactiveness, cannot be reproduced in a book.

CoNDiP will be forever incomplete: more roots, words, comments, and texts will continually be added. There will be a need for minor revisions and correction of errors.

It is our basic idea - and crucial for the success of the project - that anybody can contribute with a Nahuatl text that he or she has analyzed and translated. Precisely how this is possible is discussed on page 72.

The two dictionaries and the text component, that contain the Nahuatl language material, thus form one integral element in the system characterized by this interactiveness; however, the system comprises three other and smaller elements that handle some special functions: 1) one takes care of choice of language of glosses and communication, alphabet standards, letter functions, and information level (see pages 54-57); 2) another contains the help text (see page 48); and 3) a third contains the possibility of lifting material from the dictionary components out of the system (see pages 57-59).

Entirely new elements may be added. Some examples are: Alonso de Molina's Spanish-Nahuatl and Nahuatl-Spanish dictionaries, and a component that classes and organizes derived words according to derivational affixes.



Possibilities and options

You may lift dictionary entries out of the system, and the texts are directly available through a word-processor; both dictionary entries and texts can be printed out once they are out of the system.

But you cannot offhand change anything in the texts or entries, nor can you - even just for your own use - add comments anywhere within the system. The installation of every updated version of CoNDiP will delete the previous one, and all individual comments and additions would be deleted with it. However, if you write to us, you will influence our work and your comments will be entered.

Three different alphabet standards are offered.

For glosses and commands you can choose between English and Spanish. English is the default language of both glosses and of the commands and options.

In the Description & Manual the Nahuatl examples are given in the writing standard that most closely resembles that found in 16th century documents, (16th century Orthography); in some cases, however, a macron for long vowel and an apostrophe for the saltillo, glottal stop, may have been added, e.g. cöcoyo' 'coyotes'.

The movements, choices, and operations are controlled either with function keys, other keys, or with letters; see chapter 6, Functions and Commands. Here and in the Primer, these are written in the following way:

<F1> Function key 1

<sF1> Shift F1

<aF1> Alt F1

G letter G

Glosses are marked by inverted commas, totolin 'turkey'.



Types of information

In the Description & Manual you will find three types of information: 1) presentation of the screen: what kind of information and messages does it give and where, 2) information about the operations and options that allow you to use the system, and 3) discussions and arguments concerning the decisions and principles that are behind the two previous types of information.

The first 6 chapters present how CoNDiP functions and what principles we have followed. These chapters are purely descriptive and do not include our arguments for decisions made, and the principles are not discussed.

Discussions and references to other works and other people's opinions are found in Chapter 7. In the appropriate places in the preceding chapters there may be references to the various topics dealt with in chapter 7.

We invite reactions to the Description & Manual and to the system in general. If you have comments or can show that a decision or principle would be more pedagogical and more appropriate if changed, we will include your contribution in chapter 7 of the next version of this Manual, and it will influence our revisions of the system.

The chapters are numbered for ease of reference; they function independently and can be read in any order.



General features

Everywhere in the system the four bottom lines on the screen are reserved for practical messages; they depend on where you are in the system, so they are not always the same. Since these lines belong to the common screen lay-out and only contain simple abbreviations of some of the commands and options available in the individual components, they will not be described or explained specifically in the Description & Manual.

The HELP text, which is accessible from everywhere in the system, gives brief explanations about the Functions and Commands and other practical information.

2. Word Dictionary



Organization and information

The Word Dictionary is made up of two levels; on the top level there are two windows between which the system chooses: it either identifies the word asked for and displays it in the word entry window, or it presents in the word list window a list of words that approximately match the word asked for, or a list of homographs that all match it. Both the word entry window and the word list window occupy the top part of the screen.

The second level is governed by the individual word entry: it also has two windows, one displaying sentences in which the word from the entry occurs, the other being used for comments; these two alternate below the entry window, and they may both be empty. If neither one of them has any information, the sentence window will appear as the default window. If there is a comment, the comment window will be the default window and will appear with the word entry; the sentence window must then be invoked.

The "colored" TOP LINE on the screen gives, from left to right, the following information:

1) you are in the Word Dictionary

2) there is a Comment on the word - if there is one. 3) the number of Sentences in the CoNDiP text corpus that contain the word of the entry; this may be 0 Sentences. If there are more than xx such sentences, it will show > xx sentences.

On the right hand side of the screen, just above the two bottom lines, that are reserved for commands, a message appears every time a word is being asked for. It will say Entry: and display the word asked for, in exactly the form in which it has been typed; that will reveal possible typing errors. Under this there may be one of several other messages:

entry not found accompanied by a rejecting beep, indicates that what has been typed does not correspond to any word entry in the dictionary.

ambiguous 2 indicates that two entries - or whatever number follows ambiguous - correspond to the word that has been typed. When this message appears the two or more entries appear on the screen in a word list.



suggestion indicates that the entry that has appeared corresponds only partly to what you have asked for; there may be a difference of vowel length or glottal stop.

The message suggestion never occurs when the alphabet standard selected is 16th century Orthography.

These messages thus vary, depending on the alphabet standard in operation; for example, in 16th century Orthography toca is Ambiguous: 2, the two are toca 'follow' and toca 'bury'; in the Normalized version, however, toca is not ambiguous, it can refer only to the word 'follow', since töca 'bury' has a long vowel.



Entry window

The word entry in the entry window contains the Nahuatl word, an English and a Spanish gloss, word class, in some cases another form of the word, some grammatical information, and a Root List.

The Nahuatl word has the traditional dictionary form, with as few affixes as possible: nouns appear with the absolutive suffix in the singular, and verbs in the third person singular, with no object prefix.

The Nahuatl word is assigned to one of the following word classes: noun (n), verb (v), adjective, (adj), pronoun (pro), numeral (num), relational noun (rel), and adverb (adv).



Another form of the word that may appear in the second line just below the word is the plural ending of a noun or of a numeral, and (part of) the perfect (preterite) form of a verb. On that same line, to the right of the plural ending the possessed form is given for some nouns; there may be room only for the last part of this form; for the word tlatoani, for example, it is ocauh.

Most of the plural endings should create no problem; they are tin, me, que; some have just R which indicates reduplication, and some have R tin, or R me which means that they form the plural by reduplication plus the ending given; still others have - in addition to the ending - an asterisk which refers to a comment, in the Comment window, about an alternative plural form.

To give an example: under coyotl we find me *, which means that the plural form is coyome and that there is a comment. In the comment we learn that an alternative plural form is cöcoyo'.

The perfect (preterite) of the verbs is given in its full form only if it is very short; so, often it is only the last part of it that appears, for example ti for micti.

Some have an asterisk next to the form which refers to a comment about an alternative perfect (preterite) form, that is the case with for example the verb mati.



The type of grammatical information found in the entry window depends on the word class.

Some nouns are assigned to one of the following classes: toponym, prop.name, ethnonym, zool, bot, min, astr.

A few nouns are marked as obl.poss; that means that they are found only in the possessed form, an example is the word axca 'property'.

In the case of verbs, the grammatical information has to do with transitivity. Verbs that are marked with intr are intransitive, they take no object; those marked with tr 1 are transitive and take one object, they may also be used with the reflexive prefixes; refl (reflexive) marks only the very few verbs that are found exclusively with the reflexive prefixes, e.g. tlaloa 'run'; tr 2 indicates transitive with 2 objects, for example maca 'give'; tr 3 indicates transitive with three objects, and tr 4 transitive with four objects.

Verbs are not marked as reverentials, causatives, or applicatives, but only for transitivity and the number of objects they take.

It is characteristic of Nahuatl verbs traditionally called passives or impersonals that they have an impersonal agent: where the verb cochi 'he sleeps' is an intransitive verb with a personal agent (= subject), the derived "impersonal" verb cochihua has an impersonal agent: 'someone or people (impers.) sleep'. The verb qua is transitive and takes one object ( qui-qua 'he eats it'), qualo derived from qua also has an impersonal agent and can be translated either 'someone or people (impers.) eat(s) it' or 'it is being eaten' with no agent - i.e. impersonal agent - indicated in the translation.

imp.agent thus refers to a verb with only an impersonal agent, and formed from an intransitive verb, e.g. cochihua.

imp.ag 1 refers to a verb that is derived from a transitive verb that takes one object; the derived verb takes an impersonal agent and one other element, and is translated either with a simple passive construction with no agent or with 'someone/people (impers.) ... it', e.g. qualo.

imp.ag 2 refers to a verb derived from a transitive verb that takes two objects; e.g. techmaca 'he gives it to us', timacoque 'someone (impers.) gives it to us' or 'we are given it'.



Glosses

It has been a basic concept behind our creating CoNDiP that words can be understood only from their use in actual contexts, and the glosses are only crutches, not assigned any significant role.

There may be more than one gloss for a word, in which case they are separated by a semicolon.

There are words that we do not understand; they will have a question mark in place of a gloss; one example is mohuia, found in CF11.

Glosses in quotation marks are not real glosses; they specify the class to which a noun belongs; for example, the gloss for cencohuatl is "snake"/"culebra", whereas cohuatl is simply glossed snake; and the gloss for tlacateccatl is "title"/"título"; etc.

The last line of the entry window gives the root(s) contained in the word.

Variant words

Some words have two variant shapes, for example centli and cintli for 'corn'. Only one of them has a full entry. If you ask for the other one, you will be referred to the one with the full entry. If you look up cintli, for example, you will be told: "see centli", and you press <Return> to go to see it.



(Near) homographs

Sometimes a word list - rather than a simple word entry - will appear on the screen. This depends on the alphabet standard in operation and happens in two cases:

1) when the word you have typed is homographic with one or more other words. For example, in 16th century Orthography toca is ambiguous, it can be either the word 'follow' or the word 'bury'; in the Normalized version and in Transcription the two words are not homographic, 'bury' has a long first vowel.

2) when what you have typed corresponds only approximately to two or more word entries. This is irrelevant for 16th century Orthography since the approximate correspondence involves only differences of vowel length and presence versus absence of glottal stop. If you type, for example ahuatl, a word list with ähuatl 'oak', a'huatl 'thin thorn', and ähuätl 'wooly worm' will appear, and in the bottom right hand corner the messages will be Entry: ahuatl and Suggestion. If the alphabet standard in operation is 16th century Orthography, and you type ahuatl, you will get the same list of words, but without indications of glottal stops and vowel lengths, and the messages will be Entry: ahuatl and Ambiguous: 3.

The homographs and suggestions in the word list are accompanied by word class and glosses.



Comment

If there is a comment on the word, then it will appear in the comment window as the entry comes on the screen.

Word comments deal with: alternative plural forms of nouns (see for example coyotl, cuicani, huehue), and alternative perfect forms of verbs (see for example mati, paca, tzopeliya), transitivity (see for example aci, tzahua), the shape of the word (see for example axca, camatl), glottal stop and vowel length (see for example huepol, totomochtli), and how we analyze and understand the word (see for example toctia, metlapilli, ezhuahuacatl).



Sentence List

In the sentence window, sentences that contain the entry word are listed. They are from the CoNDiP text corpus. Every sentence is preceded by an abbreviated name, identifying the text source, e.g. CF11 for Florentine Codex, Book 11.

The Sentence List will be empty if there are no sentences in the text corpus in which the word is used, e.g. yoli 'be alive'.

The system behind the abbreviated names that identify the texts is explained in Chapter 4, Text Component. Appendix A has the abbreviated names of all the texts. They are also found in the HELP text (<F9>) and with a fuller description of the texts in the file called TEXTS.TXT.



Commands and options in the word dictionary

You enter the Word Dictionary with <F1> Word dict.

To look up a word you press <F2> Find word, type the word you want to look up and press <Return>.

You can also get into the Word Dictionary from one of the other two Components; those options are described in Chapters 3 and 4.

Commands and options that are specific to the Word Dictionary are: Go to root, Sentence List, Go to Text, Comment, Scroll comment, Scroll list of sentences, Suggestions.



Go to root

If the list of roots in the last line of the entry window has two or more roots, the leftmost one will be highlighted; if you apply <F7> Go to root, the screen picture will shift to the entry of that root in the Root Dictionary. But to see the root entry of any other of the roots contained in the word, the highlight must be shifted to that root in the line, with the left and right arrows.



See sentences, see comment

If there is a comment on a given word entry, the comment window will appear when the entry is first found; you shift to sentence window with <F6> Sent.list, and back to comment window with <F5> Comment



Comments

Long comments are scrolled with the up and down arrows. The page down key will move the comment 10-15 lines at a time. Home brings you to the top of the comment.

If you disagree with a comment or want to add to it, or if you want to contribute with a new comment, please send your suggestions or additions to us, and they will be entered in the next version of CoNDiP with your name and date.

In the comments we use 16th century Orthography unless vowel length and glottal stop are at issue; the macron for vowel length will appear as two dots unless you have changed the screen fonts; see the Installation guide in README.INS.



Sentences

In the sentence window, the word you have looked up is highlighted in every sentence, and the abbreviated name of the first sentence is also highlighted. With the up and down arrow you can shift that highlight to another sentence. The page down key will make the highlight jump 12 sentences at a time. The Home key will bring you back to the first sentence.

By pressing <F4> Go to text or <Return>, the screen will shift to the Text Component where a text passage will appear with the highlighted sentence from the Sentence List at the top.

If you want to know what functions and options there are in the Text Component, you have to read Chapter 4.

With <F1> Word dict. you get back to the screen picture that you left in the Word Dictionary.

If you want to see, consecutively, several - or all - of the sentences containing the word you have looked up, in their broader context and with a translation, you do not have to shift back and forth between the Word Dictionary and the Text Component. The function called Auto shift makes it possible: press <aF9> Alter and then <a> for Auto; this toggles Auto shift. <F4> Go to text will now bring the next sentence and its context up in the Text Component, and the next, and the next, etc.



Suggestions - (near) homographs

The function, Suggestions <F3>, lets you check if - in the word corpus - there are homographs, or near homographs, with the word entry on the screen. In identifying near homographs CoNDiP ignores the length of the vowels, the possible presence of a glottal stop, and the absolutive suffix; by that principle the following four words, for example, become near homographs: -a'hua 'scold', -ähuatl 'oak', -a'huatl 'thin thorn', -ähuätl 'wooly worm'. This is identical with the already mentioned homographs which CoNDiP volunteers if the word typed by the user does not correspond exactly to any word entry in the corpus, or if it corresponds to several.

The first word on the list of (near) homographs will be highlighted, you select by shifting the highlight with the up and down arrows, and with the <Return> key you then call its entry to the screen.



3. Root Dictionary



Organization and information



The Root Dictionary is made up of two levels; on the top level there are two windows between which the system chooses: it either identifies the root asked for and displays it in the root entry window, or it presents in the root list window a list of roots that will only approximately match the root asked for or a list of homographs that all match it. Both the root entry window and the root list window occupy the top part of the screen.

The second level is governed by the individual root entry: it also has two windows, one displaying a list of words that contain the root in the entry, the other being used for comments.

The word list window, which occupies the lower part of the screen, is never empty; the comment window appears between the root entry window and the word list window, but only if there is a comment.



The "colored" TOP LINE on the screen gives, from left to right, the following information:

1) you are in the Root Dictionary

2) there is a Comment on the root - if there is one.

3) the number of Words in the CoNDiP Word Dictionary that contain the root of the entry. If there are more than xx number of words, it will show > xx words.

Every time a root is being asked for, several messages appear just above the two bottom lines on the right hand side of the screen. This system of messages is exactly like the one described for the Word Dictionary on pages 15-16.

Root entry window The root entry in the upper window contains the Nahuatl root and an English and a Spanish gloss.

Word list window contains a list of words. What it actually displays is the first line - from the Word Dictionary - of all the word entries in which the root in question is contained, i.e. a word, an indication of word class, and a gloss.



Comment window

The window reserved for comments appears only if there is a comment on the root.

Root comments give references to other roots with related meaning, e.g. ehua - pehua, chapa - chaqua; or they deal with evidence for vowel length and glottal stop, e.g. elo 'green ear of corn', huitz 'thorn'; with the core meaning of the root, e.g. tlaça normally translated "throw", a normally translated "water"; with (near) homographs that are often confused, e.g. temi 'lie together', temi 'swell'; and with variant word forms, e.g. paçol (tapaçol 'nest'), tomi (tomi - tzomi 'fur').



Root List window

Sometimes the root list window - rather than a simple root entry - will appear on the screen. This depends on the alphabet standard in operation and happens in two cases:

1) when the root you have typed is homographic with one or more other roots, for example, in 16th century Orthography toca is ambiguous, it can be either the root 'follow' or the root 'bury'; in the Normalized version and in the Transcription the two roots are not homographic, the root 'bury' has a long first vowel.

2) when what you have typed corresponds only approximately to two or more root entries. This is irrelevant for 16th century Orthography since the approximate correspondence involves only differences of vowel length and presence versus absence of glottal stop. If for example you type ahua, a root list with a'hua 'scold', ähua 'oak', a'hua 'thin thorn', and ähuä 'wooly worm' will appear, and in the bottom right corner the messages will be Entry; ahua and Suggestion. If the alphabet standard in operation is 16th century Orthography, and you type ahua, you will get the same list of words, without glottal stops and vowel lengths, and the messages will be Entry: ahua and Ambiguous: 4.

The homographs and suggestions in the root list are accompanied by glosses.

Some words occur in two variants, for example centli and cintli for 'corn'. However, we posit only one root form, in the case of this example cen is the posited root. If you ask for a root cin, the machine will beep, and in the lower right corner of the screen the message will be Entry: cin and under that Entry not found.

It is generally advisabel to look up a root through a known word, i.e. look up the word in the Word Dictionary and shift to the Root Dictionary after having selected the root in the Root List.



Commands and options in the root dictionary

You enter the Root Dictionary with <sF1> Root dict.; or, as explained in the previous chapter under Commands and options in the Word Dictionary, you can enter the Root Dictionary via a word entry with the function, <F7> Go to root.

You can also look up a root directly. Press <sF2> Find root, type the root you want to look up, and press <Return>

Commands and options that are specific to the Root Dictionary are: Go to word, Word list, Comment, Suggestions.



Word List

In the List of Words, the top word is highlighted. With the up and down arrows you can shift the highlight to another word. The page down key will make the highlight jump 7 words at a time. The Home key brings you to the top of the list.

By pressing <sF4> Go to word or <Return> the screen will shift to the Word Dictionary, to the entry of the selected word.

If you want to see, consecutively, the entries in the Word Dictionary of several - or of all - the words in the Word List, you do not have to shift back and forth between the Root Dictionary and the Word Dictionary. The function called Auto shift makes it possible: press <aF9> Alter, and then <a> for Auto; this toggles the Autoshift. <sF4> Go to word will now bring the next word entry up in the Word Dictionary, and the next, and the next, etc.

With <sF1> Root dict. you can always get back to the screen picture that you left in the Root Dictionary.



Comment - Word List

If there is a comment on a given root entry, both the comment window and the word list window will appear on the screen when the root entry is first found; the heading of the word list window will be highlighted; that means that that is the active one. To shift the activeness to the comment window you press <sF5> Comment; and to shift it back to the word list window you press <sF6> Word list.

The up and down arrows scroll both the comment and the word list. The page down key will move the comment three lines at a time and the word list seven words at a time. The Home key will bring you back to the top of the comment or of the word list.



Suggestions

The function, Suggestions <sF3>, lets you check whether in the corpus of roots there are homographs or near homographs with the root entry on the screen. The principles behind homographs and the relationship between homographs and alphabet standards have already been described on pages 29-30.

The root that is highlighted is the one you have looked up; you can select any one of the others in the list by shifting the highlight with the up and down arrows, and with the <Return> key you then call its entry to the screen.

How to change language of the glosses, from English to Spanish, see Chapter 6, Functions and Commands.



4. Text component



Organization and information



The Text Component has three windows that are visible all the time. They display the same chunk of text: the top window gives a rendering of the text in a consistent writing, either 16th century Orthography, Normalized version, or Transcription; the middle window has the paleographic version of the text; and the bottom window offers a translation.

The "colored" TOP LINE on the screen gives, from left to right, the following information:

1) you are in the Text (Component)

2) the abbreviated name of the text shown

3) folio of the text shown

The abbreviated names are restricted to maximally 5 characters, CAUB, for example, is an abbreviation for Codex Aubin, and CF11 stands for Florentine Codex, book 11.

See Appendix A for a complete list of abbreviations for the current text corpus.

Texts in the CoNDiP text corpus are naturally divided into folios; and these are analyzed into sentences which are numbered. The folio - or page - is recognized as a unit, and the consecutive numbering of the sentences is delimited by this unit, so the first whole sentence on a folio has the number one.

Spanish loan words appear occasionally in all the texts, and many of them are linked to a word entry; some also have a root entry. They are identified by pointed parentheses, e.g. <diablo>, but these parentheses are visible only in the transcription. Longer Spanish passages are also marked by the pointed parentheses, but the words in such Spanish text passages are not linked to word entries.

In the top window, letters between parentheses, ( ), are letters that appear in the manuscript, but are considered superfluous, and letters between square brackets, [ ], have been added.

The three windows in the Text Component give the same sentences - with the same sentence numbers - in the three different versions.



Top window - consistent writing

The text version in the top window is in one of the three consistent alphabet standards: ORT, NOR, TRA.

Letters, diacritic marks, and punctuation marks are not transferred from the paleography to the consistent alphabet standards.

If you have entered the Text Component by asking for a text with <aF2>, then the number of the first sentence is highlighted.

If you have entered the Text Component via a sentence in a list of sentences that exemplify a given word in the Word Dictionary (<F4>), then that word - and not the sentence number - is highlighted in the first sentence in the top window.



Middle window - paleography

The paleography reproduces, as closely as possible, the text in the manuscript. Letters, diacritic marks, and punctuation marks that are found in the manuscripts will appear in the paleography.

The end of every original line of the manuscript is marked with a vertical line, ; and the end of every folio and side of folio is marked with a double vertical line, .

The concept of blanks between words did not exist in most Nahuatl manuscripts - there are random spaces between words and in the middle of words, but unsystematically and with no meaning. Here we do not respect the manuscript, but separate the text into traditionally recognized words.

In some texts the letter s occurs systematically in two different shapes; however, since their distribution is regular and predictable, we do not reproduce such differences in the paleography; peculiarities of this kind will be dealt with in the description of the individual text in the TEXTS.TXT file.

Some of the diacritic marks and symbols encountered in the manuscripts are not found on standard keyboards, but they must somehow be indicated, so a dummy followed by a number, 1, 2, 3, etc. represents such a symbol. A list of what the dummies stand for is found in Appendix A.

Some of the diacritic marks cannot be made to appear above the letter and are therefore shown after the letter, e.g. a~, i~, q~, etc.



Bottom window - translation

The translations are not texts in themselves, and they are not necessarily in good English, either. They follow the Nahuatl wording closely, and every sentence has its own meaningful translation.



Commands and options in the Text Component

You enter the Text Component with <aF1> Text mode, but it will be empty if you have not previously activated it.

You ask for a text with <aF2> Find text; you will be asked to type the name - i.e. the abbreviation - of the text, and then the folio. If you write zero or simply press <Return> in response to folio, then the first folio of the text will appear. Look in Appendix A for abbreviations of the texts found in the current text corpus.

You can also with <F4> Go to text enter the Text Component from the Word Dictionary via a sentence in the sentence list.

When you scroll the text with the up and down arrows, the sentences move simultaneously - one sentence at a time - in all three windows. The page down key makes the highlight jump 2 sentences at a time; the Home key brings you to the top of the folio.

With the left and right arrows you can select a word in the first sentence, and if you press the <Return> key, the screen will shift to the Word Dictionary, to the entry of the highlighted word.

This option is "undocumented"; i.e. it still needs some more work.



Notes and observations

We distinguish between notes and observations about the texts. Notes are brief, and they deal with ink stains, insertions above the line, words added in the margin, etc. They are marked by an asterisk, *.

Observations are longer; they deal with illustrations, additions, or passages that need some kind of explanation, arguments for translations that differ from those of others, and the like; they are marked by a different symbol, .

For the present version of CoNDiP, 2.1, both notes and observations are located in a file called TEXTS.TXT which also contains brief descriptions of the texts in the text corpus. You can print out the file, TEXTS.TXT.



5. Alphabet Standards

Three alphabet standards for Nahuatl are available in the system: 16th century Orthography, the Normalized version, and Trancription. A complete list of the letters and symbols used in the three Standards is found in Appendix B. When you enter CoNDiP the Nahuatl words and roots and the text in the top window of the Text Component will be in 16th Century Orthography; 16th century Orthography is the default standard, and with the command <aF9> Alter and O, N, and T, you may at any time shift to any of the three. The change of alphabet standard operates on everything in the entry windows, in the word lists and sentence lists, and in the top window of the Text Component, but it does not operate within root comments and word comments.

16th Century Orthography resembles most closely the orthography we find in the documents, but it is consistent and allows for no variation.

Normalized version differentiates short and long vowels: a vs. ä, -cälli 'raven' vs. -calli 'house'; and glottal stop is indicated, with an apostrophe: e.g. -ta'tli 'father'.

With a capital vowel the system indicates that we have no information about vowel length and possible glottal stops: e.g. -tläl-mOmOstli 'earth altar'. If you use capital vowels in looking up a word or a root you ask the system to search via all kinds of vowels, both long and short and vowels followed by a glottal stop.

In the Normalized version stems are marked by a preposed hyphen: -tläl-mOmOstli.

Some letters in the Normalized version differ in a few contexts from 16th Century Orthography:

ORT NOR English

quiqua qui-cua eats it

çacatl zacatl grass

Transcription is the orthography that least resembles what we see in the documents. In the Transcription every phoneme is represented by only one letter, and the same one in all contexts. Morphemes are marked as:

stems by a preposed hyphen: -nemi

derivational affixes by a preposed equal sign: -nemï=tia

inflectional affixes by a preposed plus: -kal+li

and reduplications are also marked specifically as such, by a preposed : +kö-koyo+', =ka-kala=ka

The following lists exemplify the differences between 16th Century Orthography and Transcription:

ORT TRA English

chantli -än+i home

cactli -kak+i sandal

quenin -kën=in how

quiqua +ki-a eats it

quicuepa +ki-epa turns it

neuctli -ne+i honey

çan -san only

cecec =sE-së=k cold

nez -nës appeared

nezque -nës+ke' appeared

tlacatl -äka+ man

tzatzi -淮'烹 shouts

huehuetl -wëwë+ drum

huauhtli -waw+i amaranth

Transcription is the master orthography in which roots, words, and texts are entered in the program, and from which the two other orthographies are mechanically derived. The Transcription determines, according to a fixed set of principles, how the orthography of the text from the manuscripts is standardized and made consistent.

The system is sensitive to the three alphabet standards and responds differently depending on which standard is in operation: TRA is prim and recognizes only the correct spelling with letters that belong in TRA, it will not recognize tl and ch. It has some tolerance of vowel length and glottal stop so that if for example you want to look up kïsa and type kisa, you will get the correct entry, but as a suggestion; if you type toka you get only the entry for the verb 'follow' since only that word is spelled exactly in that way.

The two other alphabet standards are tolerant, will find for example the word çacatl typed in any of the following ways: sakatl, sacatl, zacatl, zakatl, çacatl, çakatl. Thus for looking up roots and words ORT and NOR give much more leeway than TRA; and TRA offers more information, but of interest to fewer users.

None of the three alphabet standards recognizes anything but i for the vowel i, y for the consonant y, and o for the vowel o.

The system's sensitivity to the three alphabet standards is seen most clearly in the messages displayed when one looks up roots and words, see pages 20-21.



6. Functions and Commands



The organization of the system

The system is made up of separate elements: the core element - containing all the Nahuatl language material -consists of the two dictionaries and the text component; in addition there are other and smaller elements that handle special functions: one handles choice of language of glosses and communication, alphabet standards, letter functions, and information level; another contains the help text; and a third deals with the possibility of lifting material from the dictionary components out of the system.

Function keys from 1 through 7 operate commands within the core element, that comprises the two dictionaries and the text component. Function keys 9 and 10 operate the special functions, including leaving the system.

When you wish to read or edit the root entries in a word processor program or print them out, you will want to set tabulators, otherwise they will be illegible.

Every entry will take up two lines - the two top lines of the actual root entry - and the entries will be separated by one line.

The roots are listed alphabetically, but not entirely in agreement with the traditional order of the alphabet: the vowels a, e, i, o are the first four letters, thereafter the order is the expected, except that ch, cu, tl, and tz are the last letters in the "CoNDiP alphabet". This order of the alphabet is in operation both word initially and word internally; thus the root o 'road' will come before any root beginning with a consonant, chan 'home' will come late on the list, tzatzi 'shout' will come in the very last part of it, the root cochi 'sleep' will come after the root copal 'gum from trees', etc.

When you wish to read the root list in a word processor program or print it out, you will want to set tabulators, otherwise it will be illegible.

What is a word?

In general it is not a problem to identify words in Nahuatl; the prefixes and suffixes serve as sure signals.

However, many fixed expressions: some month names, metaphors, idioms, and the like, consist of more than one word, and since there is no level in the system between word and sentence, such expressions are not singled out or gathered anywhere. But they can of course fairly easily be located in sentences that contain one of the words in the expression.



Principles for entering words

Since the main purpose of the program is to present the words as used in texts, our original concept was to enter no word that we did not have exemplified in the text corpus. However, it turned out that the preparation of texts was far more time-consuming than expected, and if we stuck to the original concept, the number of word entries would grow far too slowly.

As a consequence we now aim at entering all the roots that are found in Molina's dictionary, and with every root the most common words formed from it.

Obviously, we also continue to enter all the words found in the texts, the roots they contain, and often also other words that contain the same root. When encountering long compounded words in the texts we enter not only those but also the simple words they are composed of.

Assuming that most users will prefer to look up both words and roots through the Word Dictionary, i.e. go via a word entry to a root entry, variation in words have not been carried over into the Root Dictionary. Therefore, even though both cintli and centli exist in the Word Dictionary (cintli with a "See centli"), there is only one root, cen, and to cin the Root Dictionary will simply respond with a snubbing beep and "Entry not found".



Sentences - Comment

Our reason for having the COMMENT appear automatically in a word entry and letting the user ask for the list of sentences is pedagogical; we assume that everyone will want to see the sentences, and that few users would look up the comment if it were not delivered for free, so to speak.



The marking of transitivity

We have decided not to use Molina's now broadly accepted way of indicating transitivity, by prefix combinations ni., nitla., nic., etc, firstly because we find that it gives insufficient information. It is not enough to know that a verb is transitive, we must also know whether it takes one, two, three, or more objects. This is important for a correct understanding of the texts, particularly since only one definite object prefix can occur with each verb. The exemplification of the verbs in the sentences illustrate the use - and thereby the transitivity - of the verbs.

Secondly, because, contrary to Molina, our aim is to keep the various uses of the same verb together in one entry so that the user freely can form his own interpretation of how the verb is used when transitive, when reflexive, etc.



Order of sentences in the Sentence List

The sentences that exemplify a given word in the Word Dictionary are listed more or less randomly, i.e. not according to any principles. This is not a problem yet since so far only words like auh and niman have unmanageably many sentences; however, it will become a problem as soon as more longer texts are entered.

We have some ideas about how to handle it: one would be that the user can ask to see sentences from only one document or only the first ten sentences from every document. But it is still unclear what solution will be chosen and how it will be implemented.



Principles for entering roots

Since the original concept of entering only words that were exemplified in the texts turned out to give too few words and therefore also too few roots, we have had to change this concept; we now aim at entering all the roots that are found in Molina's dictionary and with every root the most common words formed from it.

Obviously we continue to enter all the words found in the texts and as a consequence also the roots they contain.



Principles for root identification

The principles for analyzing words into roots and derivational affixes are still under discussion. They have not been made more systematic since the pilot project, and are still not based strictly on language history.

A few naive rules-of-thumb have guided most of the decisions: Nahuatl roots generally consist of one or two syllables, so if a word has more than two syllables it is assumed to be composed of several roots or of one root plus some derivational affix(es); in most cases the derivational affix is easily identifiable; however, there are a number of three-syllable words that are not so simply analyzed.

We are aware of possible (morphophonemic) analyses that are nevertheless not incorporated, either because they have not yet been worked out thoroughly enough, or because they somehow do not fit, or they seem of use to only few people.

Some examples are:

The vowel that occurs before the absolutive suffix tl is in some nouns never dropped, e.g. amatl 'paper', xochitl 'flower'; in some it disappears in all the other forms, e.g. xihuitl 'year'; and in others it is dropped in one form only, e.g. petlatl 'petate'. If this vowel occurs in any one form other than in the absolutive, then it is assumed to form part of the root. In all cases the vowel is recognized as belonging to the word, and not to the absolutive suffix.

For the sake of simplicity the analysis which assigns vowel length of a stem vowel to a derivational suffix is not respected; thus for example, in the word tomä=wa 'thick' the root is considered to have a long ä, tomä, and the suffix, which may be considered bearer of the vowel length, is simply recognized as wa.

Elements of words that cannot be identified as meaningful morphemes, ex. Xaltocan, (cf. Dyckerhoff & Prem 1990, p. 42.), tapaçolli 'nest', appear unidentified as such in the words.

The list of words in the Root Dictionary that contain a given root is not organized systematically according to any fixed principle. At this point the basic idea is to place the simplest words first, then the derived ones, and at the end compounds that contain more than one root, e.g.

Alphabet Standards

Originally and in working with the CoNDiP pilot project we aimed at an impossible goal, namely to establish an alphabet standard that everybody could agree about. It is impossible, firstly because some scholars want to see the words the way they look in the manuscripts, whereas others want to know about vowel length and glottal stop; and secondly because all the various modern grammars, handbooks, and dictionaries differ - a little or a lot.

Our answer to the problem was almost too obvious: give the user his own choice - or almost his own. On two major points scholars who work with Nahuatl have conflicting demands: 1) should vowel length and glottal stop be indicated or not? 2) Should the very practical linguistic symbols be accepted in order to have one letter for one phoneme, and thereby avoid tl, tz, etc.?

Our 16 Century Orthography has no diacritics and does not indicate vowel length or glottal stop. However, it is not identical with any standard in the modern handbooks, which differ not only concerning vowel length and glottal stop, but also in their rendering of the sounds s, k , and w. In our 16th century Orthography we have chosen to follow the tradition of the manuscripts from the 16th century, whereas our Normalized version follows the standard found in most of the modern handbooks. And our Transcription is meant to satisfy the demands of the linguist.

Phonetic principle

All three Alphabet Standards systematically respect the basic rules of phonetic assimilation in Nahuatl. For example, the possessive prefix im is written im before vowels and before m and p; before all other consonants and at the end of words it is written in.

Since there are numerous cases where a word final n is left out, we generaly add the missing n in the transcription without parentheses and without any specific note.



8. The Future of CoNDiP



Some of the future plans for CoNDiP deal with general upkeep, and some of it involves major new undertakings. In this connection - as elsewhere - advice and suggestions are more than welcome



Entries and texts

The language material - in the form of root entries, word entries, and text files - which is contained in the present version of CoNDiP has been entered over several years while the program and some of the basic principles went through major and minor revisions. All this material needs to be proofread and revised once more.

The text corpus must be expanded. Texts that we plan on entering are Florentine Codex Books 11 and 12, parts of Chimalpahin's works, Historia Tolteca Chichimeca, and Unos anales históricos de la Nación mexicana "1528".

The new texts do not all need to be as thoroughly analyzed as the first ones we have worked with. It is our hope that those of you who have translated Nahuatl texts will want them entered into CoNDiP's text corpus whereby they will become immediately accessible to a number of scholars and with new tools.

If you want to contribute with a text, you only need to provide us with the text in paleography or in some systematic transcription, divided into sentences numbered within every folio (or page), and a translation into Spanish, French, German, or English.



Molina

As a separate element we wish to enter all of Molina's dictionary, both the Spanish-Nahuatl part and the Nahuatl-Spanish part.



Seler's file

A year ago the Ibero-Amerikanisches Institut in Berlin asked me to evaluate Eduard Seler's Nahuatl file, which contains some 60.000 cards. I proposed to enter the whole file into CoNDiP as a separate element. The Ibero-Amerikanisches Institut has applied for money from the German Research Council for the project, and as soon as there is funding for it, we plan to begin the work, which is estimated to take several years.



Text Component

The Text Component has so far had the least thorough treatment, and we plan to give it a general overhaul.



New options

Two new options are planned, and the first steps have been taken:

in the file called Readme.Pro you can read about Sequential Search which is meant to give the user the possibility of searching words by first letters, by word class, or the like.

Another future option based on a systematic morphological analysis will let the user look up regularly conjugated word forms; the program will give possible interpretations of the word and, identify, and name the morphemes, prefixes, and suffixes included in the various interpretations.
